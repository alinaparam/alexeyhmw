One light-day from home

Voyager 1, launched in 1977, has just become the first human-made object to travel so far that even light needs a whole day to reach it.

On Wednesday, 18 November 2026, at 10:16 in the morning (UTC), a small spacecraft crossed an invisible line. Voyager 1 is now one light-day away from Earth — about 25.9 billion kilometres. Light, the fastest thing in the universe, needs a whole day to cover that distance. No human-made object has ever been this far from home.

Voyager 1 was launched from Cape Canaveral, Florida, on 5 September 1977, sixteen days after its twin, Voyager 2. The plan was simple and bold: fly past Jupiter and Saturn, take pictures and send them back. The mission was supposed to last about five years. It has now been going for more than forty-nine.

Voyager 1 reached Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn in 1980. It showed scientists active volcanoes on Io, one of Jupiter’s moons, and the thick orange atmosphere of Titan, Saturn’s largest moon. Then it just kept going. In 1990, at the request of the astronomer Carl Sagan, its camera was turned back towards home one last time. In the photo, Earth is a tiny dot, less than a pixel wide — the famous “Pale Blue Dot”. Soon after that, the cameras were switched off forever to save power.

In August 2012, Voyager 1 became the first spacecraft to enter interstellar space — the space between the stars, outside the huge bubble of solar wind that surrounds our Sun. It is travelling at about 61,000 kilometres per hour. At that speed, you could fly from Moscow to New York in less than ten minutes. Even so, it will need around 40,000 years to come close to another star.

Talking to Voyager is not easy. Its radio transmitter has about 22 watts of power, roughly the same as the light bulb in your fridge. By the time the signal reaches Earth, it is incredibly weak, so NASA listens with giant 70-metre antennas in California, Spain and Australia. And now every command needs a full day to arrive, and the answer needs another day to come back. If engineers send a question on Monday, they get the reply on Wednesday.

The spacecraft’s computers were designed in the early 1970s and have about 70 kilobytes of memory — less than a single photo on your phone. Yet the team keeps fixing them from billions of kilometres away. In November 2023, Voyager 1 suddenly started sending nonsense. After months of detective work, engineers found one broken memory chip, moved its code to other parts of the memory, and in 2024 the spacecraft was talking normally again. If they hadn’t found that chip, the mission would probably have ended.

Voyager’s electricity comes from the heat of decaying plutonium, and every year it gets about four watts less. To save energy, NASA has been turning the instruments off one by one; the latest one was switched off in April 2026. The last instruments may keep working until around 2030. After that, Voyager will go silent — but it won’t stop. It will keep flying through the galaxy for billions of years.

And it carries a message. On board there is the Golden Record, a gold-plated disc with greetings in 55 languages, music from Bach to Chuck Berry and the sounds of our planet: rain, laughter, a baby’s cry. If an alien civilisation ever found it, the record would tell them who we were. Carl Sagan compared it to a bottle thrown into the cosmic ocean.

To celebrate the milestone, NASA asked people around the world to flash the letters VGR in Morse code with their phones and flashlights on the evening of 17 November. Voyager couldn’t see them, of course. But for one night, the planet that built it was waving back.
